## 1. Ethics shopping
**Definition:** choosing, adapting, or revising ethical
principles, guidelines, codes, frameworks, or similar
standards **from a variety of available offers** in order
to **retrofit preexisting behaviors** (choice processes,
strategies) and **justify them a posteriori**, instead of
implementing or improving new behaviors by
**benchmarking them against public ethical standards.**
- In short: **selectively picking ethical guidelines to
  justify existing behavior.**
- **Analogy:** browsing guidelines like a **buffet.**
- **Outcome:** inconsistency in ethical standards,
  **diluted accountability.**

**Case: Facebook and GDPR**
- Facebook operates globally, so it faces different
  privacy laws by country.
- After the EU's **GDPR** (**2018**), Facebook adopted
  privacy measures in Europe that met GDPR's stringent
  standards.
- It did **not** extend them to users outside the EU.
- **Mark Zuckerberg testified to Congress** that all
  **2 billion** users would get the same protections as EU
  residents. Facebook's actions showed otherwise.
- It **changed its terms of service** so **1.5 billion
  non-European users** were not covered by GDPR.
- Previously, all users **outside the US and Canada** were
  governed by terms with the company's **international
  headquarters in Ireland.** After GDPR, data processed in
  Ireland fell under GDPR.
- Facebook moved users in **Africa, Asia, Australia, and
  Latin America** under **more lenient US privacy laws.**
- **Why it's shopping:** it chose the guidelines that let it
  operate with **minimal changes to data collection**
  outside the EU, instead of adopting the higher standard
  globally.

## 2. Ethics bluewashing
**Definition:** **unsubstantiated or misleading claims**
about, or **superficial measures** in favor of, the ethical
values and benefits of digital processes, products, or
services, to **appear more digitally ethical than one
actually is.**
- Related to **greenwashing** (appearing greener, more
  sustainable, or ecologically friendlier than you are),
  applied to digital ethics.
- **Example:** **underfunded or powerless advisory
  councils.**
- **Outcome:** **misleads the public, stakeholders, and
  regulators** into believing the company is acting
  ethically.

**Case: Facebook in Myanmar**
- Facebook faced severe criticism for its role in the
  **Rohingya crisis**: its platform spread **misinformation
  and hate speech** that contributed to ethnic violence
  against the **Rohingya Muslim minority.**
- In response it **commissioned an impact assessment** of
  how it contributed to human rights abuses.
- **Criticism:** the assessment had **no direct engagement
  with local Rohingya communities** (the people affected).
  It mostly involved **secondary stakeholders** (limited
  stakeholder engagement).
- After the report, Facebook made **policy changes and
  content moderation promises:** took down problematic
  accounts, **invested in AI hate speech detection,**
  engaged some civil society organizations.
- Observers said changes were about **improving public
  perception**, not solving core issues. Response was
  **reactive, not proactive.** Human rights groups said it
  failed to take **preventive measures** before violence
  escalated.
- **Evidence of bluewashing:** it announced its changes
  loudly, but their implementation and effectiveness faced
  scrutiny. It appeared more concerned with **being
  perceived as acting** than addressing **root causes.**

## 3. Ethics lobbying
**Definition:** **exploiting digital ethics to delay,
revise, replace, or avoid good and necessary legislation
or its enforcement** about the design, development, and
deployment of digital processes, products, or services.
- In short: using **ethical arguments** to **delay or weaken
  legislation.**
- **Especially dangerous:** it uses the **guise of ethical
  discussion** to influence or block needed regulation.
- **Outcome:** harmful practices stay **unregulated for
  longer.**

**Case: Google and the CCPA**
- **CCPA** (California Consumer Privacy Act): gives
  consumers more control over personal data: **know what
  data is collected, to whom it's sold, and opt out of
  data sales.**
- **Google and allies** (other tech giants) spent
  significant resources **lobbying lawmakers** to modify or
  soften provisions.
- Tactics: **lobbied to delay implementation**, sought
  **amendments creating loopholes** and **limiting consumer
  control**, aiming to make **compliance easier and
  cheaper.**
- Argument: Google was **already using voluntary privacy
  measures**, and extra regulation was **unnecessary and
  could hinder innovation.**
- **Evidence:** used **self-regulation** as an argument
  against concrete legislation; presented internal data
  protection policies as proof the CCPA was **redundant and
  overly restrictive**; tried to shape privacy law to
  match business interests.

## 4. Ethics dumping
**Definition:** term **coined in 2013 by the European
Commission** for exporting unethical research practices to
countries with **weaker legal and ethical frameworks and
enforcement.** Formally:
- **(a)** **exporting** research activities about digital
  practices, products, or services to other contexts or
  places **in ways ethically unacceptable in the place of
  origin**, **or**
- **(b)** **importing the outcomes** of such unethical
  research.
- Can be **egregious**, often **violating human rights**
  because tech is introduced where regulation is **weak or
  absent.** Practices: **surveillance without consent, weak
  or very intrusive data collection.**
- **Outcome:** **exploitation of vulnerable populations**
  used as **testing grounds**, who often lack **legal
  recourse or political power** to resist.

**Case: Chinese smart cities in Africa**
- **Huawei and ZTE** lead **smart city** projects in
  Africa: **facial recognition, AI-based surveillance,
  data analytics.**
- Many of those countries lack **robust data protection or
  privacy laws**, so deployment has **limited oversight**
  on how data is collected, stored, and used. Concerns:
  privacy, misuse of personal information, **mass
  surveillance.**
- The same tech would face **severe ethical scrutiny** in
  China or Western countries (e.g., GDPR in the EU).
- Locals become a **testing ground** without the protections
  they'd have elsewhere. Companies **grow, experiment, and
  improve** products in real-world conditions.

**Evidence of dumping:**
1. Deploying surveillance tech where **regulation is weak or
   nonexistent.**
2. **Taking advantage of missing data privacy safeguards.**
3. A **double standard:** controversial surveillance tools
   are used in less-regulated regions while **restricted or
   heavily scrutinized** in stricter countries.
- **Benefit to the company:** unrestricted access to vast
  data lets it **refine the tech and gain market
  advantage**, then **reintroduce it into more regulated
  markets**, potentially **circumventing stricter
  scrutiny.**

---

## 5. Ethics shirking
**Definition:** doing **increasingly less ethical work**
(fulfilling duties, respecting rights, honoring
communities) in a given context because the **perceived
return on ethical work is low.** (The perception is
**mistaken.**)
- In short: **reducing ethical effort where perceived
  returns are low.**
- Treating different populations with **different levels of
  ethical responsibility** causes **inequality**, mostly
  hurting **vulnerable populations.**

**Case: PredPol (predictive policing)**
*(the transcript's "Fred Pool"/"Prepo" = PredPol)*
- Used **biased training data.** Historical crime data is
  biased by **disproportionate policing in minority
  neighborhoods.** No adequate mitigation.
- Was **aware of the vicious feedback loop** but made **little
  to no effort** to assess societal impact or measure
  real-world consequences.
- **Result:** **more surveillance of already heavily
  monitored communities**, **reinforced systemic bias.**
- **Evidence:** didn't proactively correct bias or ensure
  representative, fair training data. **Knowingly used**
  biased datasets. PredPol and law enforcement **didn't
  evaluate or adjust** for the feedback loop (well known in
  predictive modeling) despite evidence of **disproportionate
  impact on marginalized communities.**
- **Why companies do it:** ethical shortcomings, especially
  around inequality, don't give **immediate financial
  gains.**
- At the heart of these cases are **real individuals and
  communities** who bear the consequences.

## The takeaway
What should guide decisions? Not just regulation or
company policy but **personal values.** Values are the
**compass** that guides actions even in challenging
situations and drives us to **ask the right questions.**
