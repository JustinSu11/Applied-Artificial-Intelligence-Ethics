# Lab 3.1 Concepts: Ethics and Bias in Machine Learning
*AAI-531 · Week 3 Lab · cross-checked against the transcript*

**One line:** Bias comes from data, society, or how we interpret
results. **Fairness metrics** measure it, **mitigation** (pre, in,
post-processing) reduces it, and there's **always a fairness vs
accuracy trade-off** with no one-size-fits-all answer.

SPD" = statistical parity difference.)*

## Ethics basics
- Two ways to think: produce the **greatest good** even with some
  harm, **or** treat ethical boundaries as **immovable barriers.**
- Ethics **evolves with us**; it mirrors current values.
- **Current Western values (10):** human welfare, ownership and
  property, privacy, freedom from bias, universal usability, trust,
  autonomy, informed consent, courtesy, environmental
  sustainability.
- **Ethical transgression:** crossing a moral boundary of those
  values (discriminating, polluting), **whether or not it's
  illegal.**
- **Ethical dilemma:** a choice between options that **each lead to
  a transgression.**

## Accountability
- Predictive models now make decisions with the capacity for
  transgressions that are **hard to trace**, so it's hard to know
  **who's accountable.**
- Accountability is essential to human morality: we need to know
  who to blame for **dignity, atonement, closure, or criminal
  prosecution.**
- "Technology has an accountability issue": **moral responsibility
  is shared.**
- **Example:** an AV hits a parked car while changing lanes. Blame
  could be on the:
  1. **Driver** (inattentive, failed to take over)
  2. **Manufacturer** (didn't properly test the obstacle-detection
     AI)
  3. **Software developer** (bug or poor training data)
  4. **Road conditions** (poor roads, unclear lane markings)
- Shows a **complex web of accountability.**

## Types of bias (3 categories)
**1. Rooted in the data itself**
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sampling bias** | Sampling of subgroups is **non-random / not representative**, or some groups **over-represented** |
| **Measurement bias** | **Faulty measurements** distort data (faulty sensors, subjective survey questions) |
| **Exclusion bias** | Certain groups' features/variables **systematically excluded** (intentionally or not) -> incomplete/skewed results |

**2. Rooted in the truths the data represents**
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Systemic bias** | **Long-standing inequities / discrimination** embedded via societal or institutional structures |
| **Structural bias** | Patterns reflecting **inequalities or hierarchies**, e.g., **wage gaps** between genders or races |
| **Prejudice bias** | **Direct reflections of stereotypes** or discriminatory behavior, e.g., biased hiring practices -> gender/racial imbalance |
| **Historical bias** | Inequities / discrimination patterns embedded in society over time |

**3. From the insights we derive from data/models**
| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conservatism bias** | Favoring existing beliefs -> **underestimating new evidence** |
| **Salience bias** | **Overemphasizing prominent** features, ignoring less obvious but important ones |
| **Anchoring bias** | Relying too heavily on the **first piece of information** (e.g., initial trends) |
| **Confirmation bias** | Interpreting data to **confirm pre-existing beliefs** |
| **Algorithmic bias** | From **data design decisions or modeling processes** |

**Explicit vs implicit:**
- **Explicit** = a person's **conscious** prejudices toward a group.
- **Implicit** = **unconscious** attitudes/stereotypes that
  unknowingly affect actions and decisions.

**Protected groups:** race, gender, age, religion, sexual
orientation, marital status, etc. Within them there are
**privileged** and **underprivileged (unprivileged)** groups; the
underprivileged are **most adversely affected.**

## Fairness metrics
**What:** tools to **assess and quantify bias**, showing how equitable
predictions are across privileged and unprivileged groups defined by
**sensitive (protected) attributes.**

**Why we need them (4):**
1. **Detect bias** (does the model favor one group?)
2. **Quantify trade-offs** (fairness vs accuracy)
3. **Guide mitigation** (pick the right technique)
4. **Promote ethical AI** (equitable decisions)

| Metric | What it measures | How to read it |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistical Parity Difference (SPD)** (aka mean difference) | **Difference** in favorable-outcome probability (unprivileged minus privileged) | **Negative = unfair to the unprivileged group.** **0 = fair** (no difference). Closer to 0 is better |
| **Disparate Impact (DI)** | **Ratio** of favorable outcomes between groups (like SPD but a ratio). Common in **legal** contexts | **1 = fair.** **< 1** = unfavorable to the unprivileged. **> 1** = favorable to the unprivileged |
| **Equal Opportunity Difference (EOD)** | Difference in **true positive rates (TPR)** between two protected groups | Quantifies the TPR disparity |
| **Equalized odds** | Does the model predict equally well for all values of a sensitive attribute for **both classes**: **TPR and the negative-class error rate the same across groups** (lecture says "negative rate") | Both must match, not just one |
| **Smoothed Empirical Differential Fairness (SEDF)** | **Intersectional** fairness: includes **all categories of the protected attributes**, not just privileged vs unprivileged. From the paper *An Intersectional Definition of Fairness* | **0 = absolute fairness**; **farther from 0 = less fair** |

- **Why SEDF:** cross-tabs of protected attributes + **Simpson's
  paradox**: a group can look advantaged/disadvantaged in
  aggregate but not when subdivided.
- **Quantify dataset bias on the training data**: it's what the
  model learns from.
- **Context matters:** which metric to use depends on the
  application.

## Mitigation: 3 phases (lists are examples, not exhaustive)
| Phase | When | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Pre-processing** | Before training (fix the data) | **Reweighing**, **Disparate Impact Remover (DIR)**, **SMOTE** |
| **In-processing** | During training (change the algorithm) | **Adversarial debiasing**, **fairness-constrained optimization** |
| **Post-processing** | After training (act on outcomes) | **Input correction**, **classifier correction**, **output correction** |

- **Reweighing:** adjusts **weights of samples** to balance
  representation across protected groups before training.
- **DIR:** **modifies features** to reduce bias but keep utility.
- **SMOTE** (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique): creates
  **synthetic samples** for underrepresented classes (class
  imbalance).
- **Adversarial debiasing:** a **primary model** predicts
  accurately while an **adversary** tries to detect bias tied to a
  protected attribute.
- **Fairness-constrained optimization:** adds **fairness
  constraints to the objective function** (e.g., equal opportunity,
  demographic parity).
- **Input correction:** adjust test inputs after training.
  **Classifier correction:** adjust the **decision boundaries**
  without changing structure. **Output correction:** modify the
  **final predictions** to meet fairness criteria.

### The two used in this module
**Reweighing**
- Applies **sample weights** by **group membership** (privileged vs
  unprivileged) during training.
- **Privileged groups get lower weights, unprivileged get higher.**
- **Doesn't modify feature values or labels**; preserves data
  integrity.

**Disparate Impact Remover (DIR)**
- **Modifies feature values** to reduce bias against unprivileged
  groups while preserving predictive utility.
- A **repair process** makes remaining features **less correlated
  with sensitive attributes.** Produces a **repaired dataset.**
- **Modifies features, not labels.**

## Trade-offs (always)
| Trade-off | Meaning / example |
|---|---|
| **Fairness vs accuracy** | Mitigation **redistributes predictive performance.** A reweighted dataset may slightly drop accuracy but improve SPD/DI |
| **Fairness vs utility** | Reducing bias can reduce a feature's usefulness; DIR may cut predictive power |
| **Fairness across metrics** | Improving **one metric can worsen another** (e.g., reducing SPD may increase EOD) |

- The "million-dollar question": fairness or accuracy? Goal isn't
  to maximize either alone; **find a balance aligned with ethical
  goals and the application context.** **No one-size-fits-all.**
- **Example:** **healthcare** may prioritize fairness over
  accuracy; **fraud detection** may stress accuracy, but fairness
  **still can't be ignored.**
- Data structure/biases + your **personal and organizational
  values** guide the choice of metrics and techniques.

## Key takeaways (lecture)
1. Bias originates from **data, societal structures, or
   decision-making processes.**
2. **Fairness metrics** quantify bias; the choice depends on goals.
3. Mitigation exists at **pre, in, and post-processing.**
4. **No one-size-fits-all**, and there's always a **fairness-accuracy
   trade-off.**
