Now that we've seen how bias can manifest, let's explore how to quantify bias using something called fairness metrics. Fairness metrics are critical tools used to assess and quantify bias in machine learning models. They help us understand how equitable a model's predictions are across different groups, particularly privileged and unprivileged groups defined by sensitive attributes. Those would be our protected groups such as gender, race, age, et cetera. You might ask, why do we need fairness metrics? Well, fairness metrics can help us identify whether a model disproportionately favors one group over another. In other words, detect bias. And also this can help us quantify trade-offs such as measuring the balance between fairness and accuracy. And fairness metrics guide mitigation efforts through helping identify appropriate bias mitigation techniques that we can employ. And finally, they help us promote ethical AI through ensuring models make equitable decisions.

Let's look at a couple different fairness metrics. Our first fairness metric is statistical parity difference. And this just measures the difference in favorable outcomes between privileged and unprivileged groups. This is sometimes also known as the mean difference, and this is just the difference between the mean probability of favorable outcomes between the underprivileged and privileged groups. And so after you calculate this, you'll get a number. And a negative number represents unfairness to the underprivileged group, and a positive number is better with a number closer to zero, representing a fair outcome with no significant difference between the privileged and underprivileged groups.

Disparate impact, this is a really important sharedness metric, and you actually see this one quite often in a legal context. And the DI is a ratio of favorable outcomes between groups. And this is almost exactly like the SPD we just mentioned, except it is the ratio instead of the difference. A DI of one represents a fair outcome between groups with absolutely no difference at all. Anything less than one means unfavorable outcomes to the underprivileged group compared to the privileged group. And over one means favorable outcomes to the underprivileged group compared to the privileged group.

These next two sharedness metrics here we'll see in classification tests. So the first one, equal opportunity difference. This is a measure of how well the true positive rates or the TPRs differ between two protected groups. So instead of ensuring equality, it goes about it by quantifying the disparity between the TPRs. And for equalized odds, this metric is used to assess whether a model is predicting outcomes equally well for all values of a sensitive attribute with respect to both the positive class and the negative class, not just one class or the other exclusively. So in other words, both the true positive rate and the negative rate should be the same for all groups.

Smoothed Empirical Differential Fairness, SEDF. This fairness metric is one of the many newer ones from a paper entitled An Intersectional Definition of Fairness. Unlike the previous two metrics, it is not restricted to the predetermined, privileged and underprivileged groups, but instead is extended to include all the categories of the protected attributes. The authors of the paper argue that the concept of fairness is particularly tricky when you have a cross-tab of protected attributes. And this occurs due to Simpson's paradox, which is that one group can be advantaged or disadvantaged in aggregate, but not when subdivided into cross-tabs. Therefore, the SEDF methodology accounts for this possibility while measuring fairness in intersectional scenarios. To interpret this metric, zero represents absolute fairness. And the farther from zero it is, the less fair it is.
