In AI ethics, bias refers to systematic errors or unfairness in AI systems that arise when data or algorithms reflect pre-existing prejudices, inequalities or assumptions. Bias in AI can lead to outcomes that favor certain groups over others, perpetuating discrimination or unequal treatment based on factors such as race, gender, socioeconomic status or even other protected attributes. Bias may occur at various stages, including during data collection, model training or deployment. And addressing bias is critical to ensure AI systems are fair, transparent and, of course, inclusive.

In AI ethics, fairness refers to the principle that AI systems should treat all individuals and groups equitably without causing unjust discrimination or favoritism. Fairness in AI means ensuring that decisions or outcomes produced by an algorithm do not disproportionately harm or benefit particular groups based on attributes such as race, gender, socioeconomic status or other protected characteristics. Fairness addresses the concerns of equal treatment. AI systems should not produce outcomes that disadvantage one group over another without valid reason.

Equitable outcomes. Results from AI should reflect fairness in distribution, ensuring that everyone has access to opportunities and resources which will help us avoid systemic inequalities. Transparency and accountability should be addressed through fairness. Fair AI systems should allow for clear understanding and accountability in how decisions are made, so their biases or unfair practices can be identified and corrected.

COMPAS, which stands for Correctional Offender Management Profiling for Alternative Sanctions, is a risk assessment tool use widely in the United States criminal justice system. Its primary function is to predict the likelihood that a defendant will commit another crime. And these predictions are made on data points such as age, criminal history and personal characteristics. And then the tool's recommendations, or their output, directly influence decisions on bail, sentencing and parole.

Now, let's look at how COMPAS works. It uses an algorithm to process data on defendants, and then what it does is it assigns them a risk score as low, medium, high. And these scores represent the defendant's likelihood of recidivism. So despite the tool's widespread use, the inner workings of its algorithm remain proprietary, meaning it's difficult for the public or even legal experts to scrutinize how these decisions are made.

Unfortunately, research has shown that COMPAS is not free from bias. A well-known study by ProPublica in 2016 revealed significant racial bias in the tool's predictions. Black defendants were often falsely labeled as high risk for recidivism, while white defendants were more likely to be classified as low risk. And this disparity raises serious ethical concerns about the fairness of using AI to guide sentencing and parole decisions.

In the case of COMPAS, the major issue that went wrong was fairness, particularly how the system treated individuals across various racial groups differently. The heart of the fairness problem in COMPAS lies in the bias data it was trained on. As we all know, many AI models learned from historical data, and if that data reflects systemic biases, perhaps such as the over-policing of Black communities or higher arrest rates for minorities, the AI system will inherently learn those patterns and then replicate them in its predictions. So in COMPAS, historical data was used to train the algorithm, and of course, it reflected the racial disparities in arrests and sentencing.

For example, Black defendants are historically more likely to be arrested or convicted due to racial profiling rather than an inherent likelihood of re-offending. So these biases became embedded in the system, meaning that the algorithm was not inherently neutral, but instead reinforced existing social inequities.

Another critical fairness dilemma raised by COMPAS is the question of equal treatment versus equal outcomes. When we look at equal treatment, we have to ask the question of should we design AI systems, such as COMPAS, to be equally accurate across different groups, ensuring that both Black and white defendants, for example, are subject to the same error rates and risks of being falsely classified? Or we can ask ourselves the question for equal outcomes. Should we focus on the fairness of the outcomes, ensuring that the real-world consequences such as incarceration rates or recidivism rates are equitable across racial groups?

What happened with COMPAS was COMPAS appeared to favor equal treatment in some ways, but because it relied on biased historical data, the outcomes were not fair for all individuals. This led to a system that disproportionately harmed certain groups, as we saw with our Black defendants, while benefiting other groups as we saw with the white defendants,. The most prominent issue with COMPAS was that it demonstrated disparate impact, meaning that the accuracy of its predictions differed across racial groups. Specifically, Black defendants were far more likely to be falsely labeled as high risk for recidivism, meaning the system predicted they would commit future crimes when they were in fact less likely to do so.

For white defendants on the other hand, they were more often falsely predicted as low risk, meaning they were judged less likely to re-offend, when in reality they had a higher likelihood of recidivism than their score indicated. This inconsistency in risk prediction was a clear manifestation of bias in the system and resulted in unequal treatment between racial groups, which is a major fairness violation in criminal justice.

In AI ethics, one of the key measures we use to ensure fairness is called disparate impact. This concept is crucial because it looks at the outcomes of an AI system rather than the intent behind it. So even if a system isn't explicitly designed to discriminate, it can still disproportionately harm certain groups based on race, gender or other characteristics. We measure disparate impact using a ratio that compares the selection rates of two groups. The equation is pretty straightforward. The disparate impact ratio is equal to the selection rate of the minority group over the selection rate of the majority group. So the selection rate is essentially the percentage of individuals from each group who receive favorable outcomes. For instance, in the case of COMPAS, it might be the percentage of Black defendants classified as low-risk compared to white defendants.

Now this ratio becomes important in legal contexts. Under US law, specifically Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, employment and other decisions that lead to a disparate impact on protected groups can be deemed as discriminatory. To assess this, courts often apply the four-fifths rule. If a disparate impact ratio is less than 0.8, it indicates that the minority group is being significantly disadvantaged. This ratio has been widely applied in areas like employment and education, and increasingly, it's being considered in AI applications like COMPAS. This legal framework emphasizes the need for fairness not just in how models are designed, but in their real-world impact as well.

In conclusion, bias in AI systems like COMPAS can have serious real-world consequences, especially when used in high-stakes areas such as criminal justice. The concept of fairness in AI requires careful consideration of disparate impact and other fairness metrics. Bias can be introduced through historical data and perpetuated by algorithms leading to unequal outcomes for certain groups. We learned from COMPAS that this case illustrates the challenges of ensuring fairness when historical data reflects societal biases, and the COMPAS tool disproportionately affected minority groups, highlighting the importance of regularly auditing AI systems. Transparency, accountability and constant monitoring are critical to mitigating bias in AI. The ethical use of AI requires balancing innovation with responsibility. Ensuring fairness in AI systems is an ongoing challenge that requires collaboration between all stakeholders.
