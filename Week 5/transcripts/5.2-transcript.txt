What is Model interpretability? Model interpretability refers to how much or how human can understand the cause of a decision or prediction made by a machine learning model. And this is crucial for understanding why a particular model made this decision or prediction, or which factors influenced its outcome. And without interpretability, machine learning models are essentially black boxes, especially for those without the inherent knowledge of their workings. And so it's really difficult to trust or even assess the decisions that they make.

So why is this important in AI ethics? Model interpretability is key to upholding the principles of fairness, accountability, and transparency in the field of AI ethics. Fairness ensures that models are not biased and don't unfairly discriminate against individuals or groups based on sensitive attributes. For example, race, gender, or socioeconomic status. Accountability enables developers, regulators, and users to hold AI systems responsible for the decisions they make, which is really critical in these high stakes areas such as criminal justice or healthcare. And transparency is about making the model's decision-making process clear so that users and developers can trust and understand the system and the system's logic.

What is SHAP? SHAP is a Python library designed to explain the output of machine learning models. It's based on the concept of Shapley values, which comes from game theory. And in game theory, each player contributes to a coalition. And Shapley values measure how much each player contributed to the total outcome. So in machine learning, we think of each feature in the model as a player. And SHAP explains how much each feature contributed to the model's prediction by considering all the possible combinations of feature values.

SHAP's role in machine learning. SHAP is model-agnostic, and that just means it can be applied to any type of machine learning model, whether it's linear regression, logistic regression, a decision tree, neural network, etc. So, SHAP provides both local and global interpretability. Local interpretability means that SHAP can explain why the model made a specific prediction for a particular instance. And then global interpretability allows us to aggregate SHAP values across many predictions to understand the overall behavior of the model, such as which features are the most important on average.

SHAP is based on several key principles, efficiency, symmetry, null, or dummy, and additivity. And these principles or axioms come from Shapley values in cooperative game theory, and they ensure that SHAP provides fair and consistent explanations. So let's go into more detail about each of these axioms.

The first principle is efficiency, and this means that the sum of the Shapley values for all features in a given prediction must equal the total difference between the actual prediction and the average prediction. For example, imagine three people share a taxi ride that costs $30. The contribution of each person 10, 12, and $8 must add up to exactly 30. So in machine learning, this means that the SHAP values across all the features must sum up to the total difference between the prediction and the baseline.

Symmetry can be explained in terms of if two features contribute equally to a model's prediction in all situations, then they should receive the same Shapley value. So in our taxi example, if person A and person B travel the same distance and contribute equally to the total cost, they should each pay the same amount. So they'll be paying $15 each, right, and a $30 fare. In a machine learning context, if two features have the same effect on the model's prediction, then they should be assigned these equal SHAP values.

The third principle null can be described as if a feature has no impact on the prediction, then the SHAP value should be zero, and that's where the null comes in. So in our taxi example, imagine person C is picked up. Remember, we have person A and person B who are splitting our $30 taxi ride equally. Well, person C comes along and they don't cause any extra distance or cost in this taxi ride. So person C should pay $0 because they didn't contribute to the cost. So in machine learning, if a feature doesn't influence the prediction at all, then it's assigned a SHAP value of zero.

And the fourth axiom, additivity or linearity. If two models are combined, the SHAP values of the combined model should be the sum of the SHAP values of the individual models. So to go back to our taxi example, if person A takes a ride that costs $10, person B takes a separate ride that costs $20, then they combine their rides for a total cost of $30, their contributions should remain 10 and $20 respectively, right? But in machine learning, if you combine the two models, the SHAP values of the combined model should equal the sum of the SHAP values from each individual model.

Why do these axioms even matter? These axioms are essential in ethical AI for ensuring fairness and transparency. For fairness, these axioms make sure that the contributions of each feature are distributed fairly and that no feature is unfairly favored. In terms of transparency, these axioms allow us to trust how the costs or the predictions are allocated, making the decision-making process clear and understandable. Let's take a quick look at the SHAP value function equation. This is derived from the Shapley values in cooperative game theory. And the function ensures that each feature's contribution is calculated as the weighted average of its contribution across all possible combinations of feature subsets. This equation takes into account on how adding or removing a feature changes the model's prediction and weights the contribution of each feature based on how it affects the outcome.

While SHAP is a really powerful tool for explainable AI, it, of course, comes with its own set of limitations. The first limitation would be the computational complexity. So SHAP values are really computationally expensive to calculate. So for complex models with many features, the number of computations required, as you can imagine, grow exponentially with a number of features, which leads to unsurprisingly high computational times and resource usage. So for large data sets or models with many features, SHAP can be really slow, especially as mentioned when used to explain many, many predictions.

The next limitation would be the global interpretability factor. SHAP is primarily designed to provide local meaning that it explains individual predictions on the [inaudible 00:11:59] level. It does offer some global interpretability tools like the summary and the beeswarm plots. It's not inherently designed to explain the overall behavior of the model. So just remember SHAP excels at explaining individual predictions. It can be less effective at providing clear global insights into how a model behaves across the entire data set.

The next limitation is dependency on feature independence. So, SHAP assumes that features are independent when calculating the impact of individual features. However, as we all are aware at this point in many real-world data sets, features are very often highly correlated. So when features are correlated, SHAP may not accurately reflect how the model is using these features leading to possible misleading interpretations. And finally, the last limitation is it doesn't correct bias. So while SHAP is great for explaining predictions, it does not correct model bias. It can highlight the fact that a feature such as race or gender is highly influential, but it does not provide the mechanisms for actually mitigating the bias. Transparency does not equal fairness. That's a very important concept. And SHAP can show biased decision making, but addressing the bias requires further action, such as retraining the model, changing the data, or applying any sort of fairness constraints.

So what can we do if we do discover that there is possible bias in our model? What are the mitigation strategies? The first one is bias audits and fairness checks. So regularly audit the model's predictions, especially for sensitive attributes such as race, gender, socioeconomic status to detect any disparate impacts. Fairness metrics such as demographic parity, equal opportunity or disparate impact ratio can be used to measure the model's fairness across these different groups. The next would be ethical data collection. If this is applicable, ensure that the training data itself is representative and free from historical or societal biases. If the data is biased, even a transparent model will continue to make these biased predictions.

Another strategy would be post-processing techniques, adjust the model's predictions to ensure fairness, for example, by enforcing demographic parity. So after a model is trained, fairness constraints can be applied to adjust outcomes for certain groups without retraining the entire model. Human oversight is another important strategy. So decisions in high-stakes areas such as healthcare or criminal justice should involve human review alongside model predictions. And human-in-the-loop systems can ensure the model decisions are subject to these ethical standards and societal laws.

And finally, fairness constraints and model training. Use algorithms and techniques that incorporate fairness constraints during model training. These techniques ensure that the models optimized not just for accuracy, but also for fairness across different demographic groups.
