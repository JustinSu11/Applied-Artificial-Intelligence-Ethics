# 4.1b K-Anonymization and Differential Privacy
*AAI-531 · Week 4 · cross-checked against the transcript*

**One line:** Removing names doesn't protect people, because
**quasi-identifiers** (zip, birthdate, gender) can be linked to
other data. Two fixes: **K-anonymization** (hide people in
groups) and **differential privacy** (add random noise). Both
**cost data accuracy.**

**Where it fits:** 4.1a showed Netflix being re-identified.
This lecture is the toolbox for preventing that.

# LAYER 1: LEARN IT

## The big idea
Privacy and usefulness pull in opposite directions. Every
technique here makes data **safer** by making it **blurrier.**
Your job is to pick *how blurry* for the situation.

**Hook:** Privacy is a **blur filter** on a photo. More blur,
safer faces, harder to see what's going on.

## 1) Direct vs quasi-identifiers
**Problem:** which columns actually give a person away?

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct identifier** | Points to one person **by itself** | name, SSN, driver's license, passport, email |
| **Quasi-identifier** | Not unique alone, but **unique in combination** | date of birth, zip code, occupation, marital status, gender, ethnicity |

**Hook:** A direct identifier is your **name tag.** Quasi-
identifiers are **clues**; enough clues together = a name tag.

**Trap:** deleting direct identifiers feels like anonymizing.
It isn't. The quasi-identifiers remain.

## 2) Why it matters: Sweeney's experiment
- 1990s: Massachusetts released **de-identified medical records**
  (no names) for research. They kept **zip, birthdate, gender.**
- **Latanya Sweeney** matched them against **public voter
  records** (names + zip + birthdate + gender).
- She identified **Governor William Weld's** medical records:
  few people in his zip shared his birthdate and gender.
- Headline finding (per lecture): **80%** of the US population
  could be identified with just **zip + birthdate + gender.**
  *(Lecture says 80%. Sweeney's study is usually cited as about
  87% (my addition). Use 80% for the quiz.)*

## 3) K-anonymization
**Problem it solves:** make each person indistinguishable from
others on the quasi-identifiers.

**Definition:** every person looks the same as at least **k-1**
others on the quasi-identifiers. So every combination appears
**at least k times.** Bigger k = safer.

**Hook:** Hide in a **crowd of k.**

**The 3 steps:**
1. **Identify the quasi-identifiers** (age, gender, zip).
2. **Group and count** records by those columns. Each
   combination must appear **at least k times.** Choose k
   (3, 5, 15...). *Smaller k = easier to re-identify.*
3. **Generalize or suppress** until the groups are big enough.

**Two tools:**
- **Suppression:** remove or mask values or whole records.
- **Generalization:** broaden specific values into ranges.

**Worked example (lecture's, rebuilt and run, k = 3):**
| Step | What we do |
|---|---|
| Start | name, SSN (direct), gender, zip, age, diagnosis |
| Suppress | drop name and SSN |
| Generalize zip | mask last digit: 12345 -> 1234* |
| Generalize age | bucket into 30-39 |
| Generalize diagnosis | hypertension, diabetes -> **chronic condition**; asthma, COPD -> **respiratory issue** |
| Count combos | (M, 1234*, 30-39, respiratory) = **3**; (F, 1234*, 30-39, chronic) = **3** |
- Every combination appears 3 times -> **3-anonymous.** (verified)

**Trap:** the lecture's data is rigged so everyone falls in the
same bucket. Real data needs **more** generalizing, which costs
more accuracy.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Background knowledge / auxiliary information attacks:** an
  attacker with extra info about someone can still pick them out.
  Worse when public datasets overlap.
- **Lower utility:** wide age ranges and masked zips make data
  less precise.
- Less effective for **complex datasets with diverse attributes.**

## 4) Differential privacy (DP)
**Problem it solves:** k-anonymity breaks when attackers link
other datasets. DP gives a guarantee even then.

**Idea:** add **random noise** to results so that **one person
being in or out of the data barely changes the output.** An
attacker can't tell if you were included.

**Hook:** A **fuzzy scale**. Whether or not you step on, the
reading wobbles more than your weight.

**Success test (lecturer):** including or excluding **any single
individual** must not significantly change the result, for **any**
individual in **any** dataset.

**The Laplace mechanism (the common method):** add noise drawn
from a **Laplace distribution** to the answer.
- **Sensitivity:** how much **one person** can change a query's
  result. For a **count** it's **1** (add or remove one person).
- **Epsilon (e):** the **privacy budget.** Controls how much noise.
- Noise scale = **sensitivity / epsilon.**

**Small worked example (from the lab numbers, verified):**
- Sensitivity 1. Epsilon 0.1 -> noise scale **10.** Epsilon 0.5
  -> **2.** Epsilon 1.0 -> **1.**
- A true count of 111 might come out as 101.4 at e = 0.1: a
  **-8.6%** error. At e = 1.0 it came out 111.3 (+0.3%).
- Why it hides you (my addition): counts of 111 vs 110 (you in
  or out) produce outputs whose odds differ by at most a factor
  of e^0.1 = **1.105** at e = 0.1. Nearly indistinguishable.

**Epsilon rule (memorize):**
| Epsilon | Noise | Privacy | Accuracy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Small** | more | **stronger** | lower |
| **Large** | less | weaker | **higher** |

**Hook:** **Small epsilon = small leak** (tight budget).

**Choosing epsilon (application-specific):**
- **Healthcare:** privacy critical -> **smaller epsilon.**
- **Consumer behavior analysis:** privacy concerns lower -> a
  **larger epsilon** may be fine.
- No one-size-fits-all.

**Cumulative privacy budget:** every query on the same dataset
**spends** budget. Total privacy loss **adds up**, so
organizations must **track cumulative epsilon.**

**Trap:** more privacy is not free. It always costs accuracy,
and it costs **more for small groups** (see the lab).

## 5) Side by side
| | **K-anonymization** | **Differential privacy** |
|---|---|---|
| How | group + generalize/suppress | add calibrated noise |
| Pros | **simple**, easy to implement, well established in regulation, widely used in **healthcare and public sector** | **strong guarantees** even when data is released publicly, protects against **auxiliary (linked) data** |
| Cons | vulnerable to **re-identification via external sources**; weaker on **complex, diverse** data | noise **reduces accuracy**; **harder** to implement, needs **specialized expertise and resources** |

## Big picture recap
- Names are not the problem; **combinations of clues** are.
- K-anonymity hides you in a crowd but can be beaten by outside
  knowledge.
- DP hides your presence with noise; epsilon sets the blur.
- Both trade **privacy for utility.** Pick by context.

# LAYER 2: QUIZ REFERENCE (nothing lost)

## Identifiers
- **Identifier:** information in datasets that can reveal an
  individual's identity.
- **Direct identifiers:** unique attributes that **directly point
  to an individual without additional data.** Examples: **name,
  Social Security number, driver's license number, passport
  number, email address.**
- **Quasi-identifiers:** attributes that are **not uniquely
  identifying alone** but **can become identifying when combined
  with other quasi-identifiers.** Examples: **date of birth, zip
  code, occupation, marital status, gender, ethnicity.**

## K-anonymization
- Ensures each individual is **indistinguishable from at least
  k-1 other individuals** based on quasi-identifiers.
- Goal: protect against **re-identification attacks** by
  **reducing uniqueness** in sensitive datasets (e.g., healthcare,
  personal data).
- **Steps:** (1) identify quasi-identifiers, (2) group records
  by them with frequency count so each combination appears **at
  least k times** (k = 3, 5, 15... whatever is acceptable;
  **smaller k = easier to re-identify**), (3) **generalize or
  suppress** (broaden categories).
- **K-3 anonymous:** each record cannot be distinguished from
  **at least two other records**; every unique quasi-identifier
  combination has **at least three records.**
- **Suppression:** removes or masks specific values or entire
  records.
- **Generalization:** broadens specific values into more general
  categories or ranges.
- **Masking** (zip): drop the last digit. **Age buckets:** e.g.,
  30-39. **Diagnosis broadened:** hypertension and diabetes ->
  chronic condition; asthma and COPD -> respiratory issue.
- Lecture's example: male, zip 1234*, 30-39, respiratory issue
  appears **3 times**; female, 1234*, 30-39, chronic condition
  appears **3 times.**
- **Limitations:** vulnerable to **background knowledge attacks**
  (adversary has extra info on individuals; worse with public
  datasets that overlap); **reduces data utility.**

## Sweeney experiment
| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Researcher | **Latanya Sweeney** |
| Era | **1990s** |
| Data | Massachusetts de-identified medical records (state insurance database) with **zip, birthdate, gender** |
| Linked to | **public voter registration records** (names + zip + birthdate + gender) |
| Famous result | identified **Governor William Weld's** records |
| Key finding | **80%** of US population identifiable by **zip + birthdate + gender** (per lecture) |
| Impact | showed inadequacy of simple anonymization; led to **differential privacy** |

## Differential privacy
- Designed for this problem: **multiple databases, each holding
  small pieces of anonymized information, can together identify
  an individual.**
- Introduces **random noise** to dataset queries so any single
  individual's data is **not easily distinguishable.**
- Aim: impossible to tell from the output **whether an
  individual's data was in the original dataset.**
- Common mechanism: **Laplace mechanism**, adds noise (*transcript
  "[inaudible] noise" = Laplace noise*).
- **Epsilon:** metric of **privacy loss** at a differential
  change in the data; basically the **maximum distance between a
  query on database X and the same query on database Y.**
- **Sensitivity:** impact a change in the underlying dataset can
  have on a query's result.
- Laplace mechanism = a **general-purpose, additive noise**
  mechanism.
- **Privacy budget** (epsilon): controls the **trade-off between
  privacy and accuracy.**
  - **Smaller epsilon:** more noise, **stronger privacy, lower
    accuracy.**
  - **Larger epsilon:** less noise, **higher accuracy, weaker
    privacy.**
- **Cumulative privacy budget:** multiple queries -> budget
  accumulates -> total privacy loss increases; **monitor
  cumulative epsilon.**
- **Challenges:** complex implementation (mathematically
  calibrated noise); choosing epsilon is hard and **domain
  specific**; privacy vs utility trade-off.

## Pros and cons table (lecture)
- **K-anon pros:** simple to understand and implement; around for
  a while, **established in privacy regulations**; used a lot in
  **healthcare and public sector** data.
- **DP pros:** **strong privacy guarantees**, even when data is
  publicly released; protects against **auxiliary information**
  (external datasets linked).
- **K-anon cons:** **vulnerable to re-identification with
  external sources**; **less effective for complex datasets with
  diverse attributes.**
- **DP cons:** noise **reduces data accuracy**; **harder to
  implement**, needs **specialized expertise and resources.**
