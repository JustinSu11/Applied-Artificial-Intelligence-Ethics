# 4.1a Privacy, Safety, and Security (Netflix Prize)
*AAI-531 · Week 4 · cross-checked against the transcript*

**One line:** AI runs on **personal data**. **Privacy** = who gets
to see it, **safety** = the system behaves, **security** = nobody
breaks in. And "anonymized" data is **not** safe by default:
Netflix proved it.

**Where it fits:** Weeks 1 to 3 asked "what is ethical AI?" and
"is it fair?". Week 4 asks "what happens to the **data about
people** that AI feeds on?" Next lecture (4.1b) gives two fixes:
**K-anonymization** and **differential privacy.**

# LAYER 1: LEARN IT

## The big idea
Data is the fuel of AI, but unlike oil, **data is about
people** (habits, preferences, identity). If it leaks, people
get hurt. So the more data a company collects, the bigger the
duty to protect it.

**Hook:** Oil spills hurt nature. **Data spills hurt people.**

## 1) Three principles (don't mix them up)
**Problem they solve:** AI can fail people in three different
ways, and each needs its own protection.

| Principle | Plain words | Fails when... |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy** | Data is collected, stored, used in ways that **respect people's rights** | Your data is used or shared without your say |
| **Safety** | The AI **works reliably** and doesn't cause unintended harm | It malfunctions or gets misused and someone is hurt |
| **Security** | The AI is **protected from hacking or manipulation** | An attacker takes it over or poisons it |

**Hook:** **P**rivacy = who *sees* it. **S**afety = does it
*behave*. **S**ecurity = who can *break in*.

**Trap:** security is a tool for privacy, but they're not the
same. A perfectly secure vault can still hold data a company had
no right to collect (privacy failure).

## 2) The tension: companies vs. people
- **Companies** want huge datasets to improve algorithms and
  stay competitive. More data also means **more risk** to manage.
- **People** want their information safe and expect it to be
  handled responsibly. They increasingly demand protection.
- Result: a constant tug of war. Data is valuable to companies,
  but **deeply personal** to individuals.

## 3) Why "just read the privacy policy" fails
**Problem:** the system puts the burden on you.

**Worked example (McDonald and Cranor, Carnegie Mellon):**
- If Americans read **every** privacy policy they meet, it takes
  about **244 hours per person per year.**
- Check: 244 h x 60 min / 365 days = **40.1 min per day.**
  (verified)
- Priced as paid work: about **$781 billion a year** for the US.

**Why it matters:** policies are long and full of legal jargon,
so real informed choice is **impossible** for ordinary people.
It favors people with expertise or resources.

**Hook:** If reading the fine print is a second job, consent
isn't really consent.

## 4) Anonymized does NOT mean safe: Netflix Prize
**Problem it shows:** companies think "we removed the names, so
it's private." Often it isn't.

**The story (2006):**
1. Netflix runs a public contest: beat its recommendation
   algorithm by **10%.**
2. To help, it releases **100 million+ ratings** from
   **480,000 users** on **17,000+ movies.**
3. It "anonymized" by removing usernames and replacing some
   ratings with synthetic ones.
4. But it kept **user IDs, movie titles, ratings, and
   timestamps**, so each user had a **unique viewing pattern.**
5. Researchers (Narayanan and Shmatikov, UT Austin) ran a
   **linkage attack:** matched the "anonymous" Netflix data
   against **public IMDb** reviews and re-identified people.
6. Result: a **lawsuit**, and pressure toward stricter laws.

**Tiny example of a linkage attack (my addition, illustrative):**
- Netflix file: user #123 rated 5 obscure films on specific
  dates.
- IMDb: "Sam R." publicly reviewed the same 5 films on nearly
  the same dates.
- A pattern that rare = user #123 is almost certainly Sam R.
  Now the rest of #123's ratings (maybe sensitive) belong to Sam.

**Hook:** Your **pattern** is your fingerprint. Removing your
name leaves the fingerprint.

**Trap:** "No names, no addresses, no account numbers" does not
equal anonymous. Data that is unique per person can be
**cross-referenced** with other datasets.

## 5) How to build safer systems
- **Robustness against privacy attacks:** assume attackers will
  combine datasets. Use protections like **statistical noise**
  (differential privacy, next lecture) or **limit what you
  release.**
- **Watch for malicious use:** a tool built for one purpose can
  be repurposed and violate privacy.
- **Fallback plans for unknown risks:** privacy breaches can
  happen in unexpected ways, so plan for them. Examples:
  **regular privacy audits**, **data usage restrictions**,
  **periodic checks** to catch problems early.

## 6) Law is the floor, not the ceiling
- The Netflix case helped drive **GDPR** and **CCPA**, which
  require **better anonymization** and give users **more control**
  over their data.
- But the lecture asks "is it enough?" Answer: **compliance is
  essential but not sufficient.** Professionals must also use
  **ethical practice**: always weigh re-identification risk.

## Big picture recap
- AI needs data, data is personal, so privacy, safety and
  security are in constant tension with business goals.
- Consent through reading policies doesn't work at human scale.
- Removing names is not anonymization (Netflix).
- Defend with noise, limited release, audits and fallbacks.
- Obey the law (GDPR, CCPA) **and** go beyond it.

# LAYER 2: QUIZ REFERENCE (nothing lost)

## Definitions (lecturer's wording)
- **Privacy:** ensures data is **collected, stored, and used in
  ways that respect individual's rights.**
- **Safety:** ensuring AI systems **operate reliably and do not
  cause unintended harm to individuals, society, or the
  environment.** Includes **designing, testing, and deploying**
  AI to minimize risks of **malfunction, misuse, or harm** to
  users and communities.
- **Security:** **protecting AI systems from hacking or
  manipulation**, which could lead to **malicious use or
  unintended harmful outcomes.**
- Course framing: "Ethical **Principles of Privacy and Safety**"
  (security is the third piece).

## "Data is the new oil"
- Highlights how **valuable** data is in today's digital economy.
- Just as oil fueled the Industrial Revolution, **data powers the
  AI revolution.** Data is a **requirement for all AI systems.**
- Needed so AI can **learn, adapt, improve**, make **accurate
  predictions**, understand patterns, **personalize** experiences.
- **Unlike oil, data has a personal dimension:** habits,
  preferences, identity.
- Harms if mishandled: **privacy breaches, identity theft.**
- Companies rely on large-scale data to **enhance algorithms and
  keep a competitive edge**; more data = **more risk** managing
  and securing it.

## Privacy attacks and defenses (lecture order)
1. **Robustness against privacy attacks:** even **anonymized data
   can be re-identified when combined with other datasets.**
   Defenses: **statistical noise (differential privacy)**, **limit
   data release.**
2. **Mindful of malicious use:** tools built for specific
   purposes can be **re-appropriated or modified.**
3. **Fallback plans for unknown risks:** **regular privacy
   audits, data usage restrictions, periodic checks.**

## McDonald and Cranor: *The Cost of Reading Privacy Policies*
| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Authors | **Aleecia McDonald, Lorrie Faith Cranor** (Carnegie Mellon) |
| Time to read every policy | about **244 hours/year per person** |
| Per day | about **40 minutes** |
| Economic cost (US, if paid labor) | about **$781 billion/year** |
- Issues: **accessibility and complexity**; policies are long,
  full of **legal jargon.**
- Design puts an **"impossible burden"** on the average consumer.
- Favors those with **advanced knowledge or resources.**

## Case: Netflix Prize
| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Year launched | **2006** |
| Goal | **at least 10% improvement** over Netflix's algorithm |
| Dataset | **100+ million ratings, 480,000 users, 17,000+ movies** |
| "Anonymization" | **removed usernames**, **replaced a portion of ratings with synthetic ratings** |
| Still included | **user IDs, movie titles, ratings with timestamps** (unique viewing patterns) |
| Not included | names, addresses, account numbers (direct identifiers) |
| Attack | **linkage attack**, combining **IMDb data** with Netflix data |
| Researchers | UT Austin computer scientists (named in transcript as "Arman and Vitaly") |
| Outcome | **lawsuit**; influenced **GDPR** and **CCPA** |
- *Transcript typos: "IMBD" = IMDb; "Arman and Vitaly" =
  Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov (my addition).*
- Ethics questions raised: Should companies release user data,
  even anonymized? How much responsibility for keeping privacy?

## Regulation
- **GDPR** (General Data Protection Regulation) and **CCPA**
  (California Consumer Privacy Act): require **better data
  anonymization** and give users **more control** over personal
  data.
- Lecture asks: "**But is it enough?**"

## Key lessons (lecture's closing list)
- Privacy, safety, security: **crucial, with complications.**
- **Anonymized data can still be dangerous.**
- Always consider the **risks of re-identification** when
  handling or sharing user data.
- **Regulatory compliance is essential, but we also need ethical
  practices** to truly protect user privacy.
